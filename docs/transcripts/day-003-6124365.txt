Alex: We were sitting in a capital allocation committee last Thursday reviewing an expansion for our health sciences administrative offices. It was twenty-five thousand square feet in an off-campus suburban building. The development team was split right down the middle. Half the room wanted a turnkey delivery because our internal facilities staff is stretched paper-thin and material costs are volatile. The finance director pushed back hard, demanding an eighty-dollar-a-square-foot tenant improvement allowance instead so we could control our own finishes and timeline. That debate stalls institutional projects constantly. This is Director's Desk, Day 3.
Morgan: That tension between turnkey and a stated allowance is one of the most common friction points in institutional leasing. On the surface, it looks like a simple preference between convenience and design control. In reality, it is a legal and financial decision about who absorbs construction risk, who finances the improvements, and what your rent really pays for across the full occupancy cycle.
Alex: Let us start with turnkey. Why does half the room view turnkey delivery as a silver bullet, and where does that thinking fall apart?
Morgan: Turnkey sounds simple. The landlord promises to deliver the space fully built out according to an agreed-upon space plan and work letter, at their sole expense. The obvious appeal for an institutional tenant is that the landlord bears the risk of cost overruns. If labor spikes, drywall costs jump fifteen percent, or the mechanical contractor discovers an unexpected ductwork reroute, the landlord absorbs that cost.
Alex: But the landlord is not a charity. How do they hedge that risk?
Morgan: They hedge it in two ways. First, they build a healthy contingency into the base rent you pay for the next ten years. Second, if your initial work letter is vague, they will cut every possible corner on materials and fixtures to preserve their margin. If you ask for turnkey without an exhaustive set of architectural specifications, lighting schedules, carpet standards, and acoustic requirements attached as an exhibit to the lease, you are handing the landlord a blank check to install builder-grade finishes. In healthcare or higher education, where you have specific acoustic dampening, data drops, or durable infection-control surfaces, a generic turnkey work letter is dangerous.
Alex: Which brings us to the alternative: a stated tenant improvement allowance. Usually expressed as twenty, fifty, or eighty dollars a square foot. How does the risk transfer here?
Morgan: With a TI allowance, the financial risk swings entirely back to the tenant. The landlord gives you a fixed credit. If your bids come in at ninety dollars a square foot on an eighty-dollar allowance, your institution writes a check for that ten-dollar difference. You also have to watch the measurement basis. An eighty-dollar allowance on rentable square feet gives you more total cash than eighty dollars on usable square feet. If you have a twenty percent core factor, that distinction is significant on a large footprint.
Alex: What most people miss is that whether it is turnkey or a stated allowance, that money is rarely free.
Morgan: It is almost never free. Landlords look at TI dollars as tenant financing. When a landlord grants you an eighty-dollar-a-square-foot allowance on a ten-year lease, they are not taking a loss on day one. They are amortizing that eighty dollars into your base rent, typically at an implicit cost of capital between seven and ten percent.
Alex: Walk through that math with real numbers.
Morgan: Take a ten-year lease. If a landlord gives you sixty dollars a square foot in tenant improvements and amortizes that at eight percent over ten years, that build-out capital translates to roughly eight dollars and seventy cents per square foot per year in debt service baked directly into your base rent. If your face rent is thirty-five dollars a square foot, twenty-six dollars and thirty cents is the actual building rent, and eight dollars and seventy cents is you paying off the landlord's loan for your walls, carpet, and wiring.
Alex: That leads directly to what you call the renewal cliff. This is the single biggest trap I see in long-term lease management.
Morgan: It burns institutional tenants every single day. Year ten arrives. Your initial term is expiring. Your facilities team likes the location, and you exercise your five-year renewal option, or you negotiate an extension. The broker comes in and says market rents in the submarket have gone from thirty-five to thirty-eight dollars a square foot, or the lease stipulates the renewal rent is ninety-five percent of fair market value. The tenant agrees to thirty-eight dollars. What did they just do?
Alex: They agreed to keep paying for the drywall they already paid off during the first ten years.
Morgan: Precisely. In the initial term, eight dollars and seventy cents of that rent was amortizing improvements that are now fully depreciated and paid for. If you do not strip that amortized capital cost out of the baseline before you negotiate the renewal rate, you are effectively agreeing to finance the landlord's asset a second time. At renewal, your baseline conversation has to start from raw space value, not an unadjusted continuation of a rent number that carried heavy upfront capital financing.
Alex: There are two other lease terms that quietly bleed cash if you do not catch them in the letter of intent: use-it-or-lose-it deadlines and landlord management fees.
Morgan: Let us take use-it-or-lose-it first. Standard landlord lease drafts routinely state that any portion of the tenant improvement allowance not drawn within six, nine, or twelve months of lease execution is forfeited. In an institutional setting, permits stall, clinical designs get revised, or supply-chain delays push back the final phase. If you hit that deadline, the remaining allowance vanishes into the landlord's pocket, but your base rent remains inflated because the amortization was locked into the lease schedule.
Alex: How do we fix that language in the LOI?
Morgan: You require that any unused tenant improvement allowance converts into a base rent credit. If you leave five dollars a square foot on the table, that balance automatically offsets month thirteen and month fourteen base rent until fully exhausted. You never allow TI dollars to expire.
Alex: And what about the landlord construction management fee?
Morgan: If the tenant manages their own build-out using their own general contractor, the landlord will often insert a construction coordination, supervisory, or review fee. It typically starts in the draft at three to five percent of the total hard and soft costs. If you are doing a two-million-dollar build-out, that is a hundred thousand dollars paid to the landlord just to review submittals and let trades into the freight elevator.
Alex: What is the acceptable institutional threshold?
Morgan: Zero to one percent for third-party institutional projects, or capped at actual out-of-pocket costs for external structural or engineering reviews. If the landlord insists on managing the project, you cap their construction management fee at three percent max. Anything above that is a stealth profit center for their property management arm funded directly out of your capital.
Alex: Let us run our daily two-question check.
Morgan: First question. You are negotiating a five-year lease for an eight-thousand-square-foot clinic. The landlord offers thirty dollars a square foot in base rent with no TI, or forty dollars a square foot with fifty dollars in TI. What is the implied annual cost of that financing?
Alex: The tenant is paying ten dollars more per year in base rent to receive fifty dollars upfront. Over five years, that extra ten dollars equals fifty dollars in nominal cash. You are paying ten dollars a year on a declining balance that averages twenty-five dollars over the term. That equates to an effective interest rate of roughly fifteen percent per year. It is wildly expensive capital compared to institutional debt.
Morgan: That is clean CCIM math. Second question. Your project finished under budget, leaving ten dollars a square foot of unused TI. The lease states the funds must be drawn within six months of substantial completion, which passed thirty days ago. The landlord claims the money has reverted. Where did the lease negotiation fail, and what is the institutional remedy?
Alex: The negotiation failed at the LOI stage by omitting an automatic rent-credit conversion clause and leaving a strict forfeiture deadline. The remedy now is to audit the landlord's construction management fees and tenant-reimbursable charges to recover cash, while insisting on an amendment that offsets that remaining allowance against future base rent.
Morgan: Spot on. Never accept a forfeiture clause on money you are actively paying back in your lease rate. Alex, give us today's one-sentence Director's rule of thumb.
Alex: Always strip amortized build-out costs out of base rent at renewal and negotiate rent-credit rights for unused TI dollars.
