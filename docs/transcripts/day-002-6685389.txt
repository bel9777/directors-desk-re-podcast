Alex: You are sitting with your health system's chief financial officer, reviewing the annual operating expense reconciliation on a forty thousand square foot off-campus outpatient center. The budget projected three hundred thousand dollars in common area maintenance. The invoice from the landlord lands at three hundred and eighty-five thousand dollars. The CFO looks across the table and asks how our operational costs spiked twenty-eight percent when our lease proposal promised a five percent cap. If you do not know how your CAM pool is defined, how the gross-up was calculated, or whether that cap was cumulative, you have no answer. This is Director's Desk, Day 2.
Morgan: That reconciliation meeting happens every April across corporate and institutional real estate. Operating expense pass-throughs can erode millions of dollars of operating margin over a ten-year lease if your lease language leaves the barn door open. Let us start at the foundation: how your proportionate share of the building is determined.
Alex: The formula looks simple on paper. Your proportionate share is your rentable square feet divided by the building's rentable square feet. If you occupy twenty thousand square feet in a one hundred thousand square foot medical office building, your share is twenty percent.
Morgan: That is how it should work. Here is the first trap. Look at the denominator in the lease draft. Landlords frequently write that the tenant pays a proportionate share where the denominator is the leased square feet or the occupied square feet of the building, rather than the total rentable area. If that one hundred thousand square foot building is only sixty percent occupied, your twenty thousand feet divided by sixty thousand leased feet suddenly makes your share thirty-three point three percent instead of twenty percent. You end up paying the operating costs of the vacant space. Your lease must state that the denominator is always the total rentable square footage of the building, regardless of occupancy.
Alex: That leads straight into gross-up provisions. If a building is half empty, the landlord is not running the air conditioning or paying janitorial crews for the vacant suites. How does a gross-up protect both parties without turning into a windfall for the property owner?
Morgan: A gross-up clause allows the landlord to adjust variable operating expenses to reflect what those costs would have been if the building were fully occupied, typically set at ninety-five or one hundred percent. Variable expenses are items driven by tenant occupancy, like common area electricity, water, trash removal, and janitorial services. Fixed expenses, like real estate taxes, hazard insurance, and structural roof maintenance, do not change whether the building is ten percent occupied or ninety-five percent occupied, so they are never grossed up.
Alex: The economic logic makes sense. If a building is eighty percent occupied in year one and reaches ninety-five percent occupied in year two, the actual utility bills will rise simply because there are more people in the building. Without a gross-up, a tenant on a full-service gross or base-year lease would see a massive escalation over their base year that has nothing to do with inflation.
Morgan: That brings us to the subtle trap that catches even seasoned portfolio directors: inconsistent base-year gross-ups. In a base-year or modified gross lease, if the landlord grosses up variable expenses in year two to ninety-five percent, but fails to gross up your base year because occupancy happened to be seventy percent back then, your base year is artificially depressed. That creates an artificial spike in pass-throughs every single year of the lease term. The rule is absolute: if expenses are grossed up in comparison years, the base year must be grossed up using the exact same formula and occupancy threshold.
Alex: Now let us talk about caps, because that is where the CFO in our opening scenario got burned. The lease had a five percent cap, but the invoice showed a twenty-eight percent jump. How does that happen?
Morgan: It happens because the lease failed to distinguish between controllable and uncontrollable expenses, or the cap was structured as cumulative rather than non-cumulative. In commercial leasing, operating expenses fall into two buckets. Uncontrollable expenses are dictated by third parties or nature: real estate taxes, special assessments, building property insurance, utility rates, and snow and ice removal. Landlords will almost never agree to cap uncontrollable expenses. Everything else is controllable: landscaping, routine exterior maintenance, janitorial contracts, security, window washing, and management fees.
Alex: When we negotiate a five percent cap on controllable operating expenses, we have to fight for non-cumulative. Spell out the real math behind cumulative versus non-cumulative caps.
Morgan: Say your controllable operating expenses start at ten dollars a square foot in year one. In year two, costs stay flat at ten dollars. In year three, costs stay flat at ten dollars. In year four, the landlord replaces the landscaping vendor and service costs jump to thirteen dollars. Under a non-cumulative five percent cap, the landlord can only charge you five percent above what you paid in year three, meaning ten dollars and fifty cents. The landlord eats the remaining two dollars and fifty cents. Under a cumulative cap, the landlord banks the unused five percent cap from year two and year three. That compound cap allows them to hit you with a fifteen to sixteen percent increase in year four. Always insist that controllable caps are non-cumulative, based on actual prior-year expenditures.
Alex: Let us look at what gets sneaked into that CAM pool in the first place: capital expenditures and property management fees.
Morgan: Capital expenditures belong on the landlord's balance sheet, not in your operating expense reconciliation. Your lease must explicitly exclude capital improvements, structural replacements, foundation work, and roof replacements. There are only two narrow exceptions an institutional tenant should ever entertain. First, capital improvements required by government regulations enacted after the lease commencement date, such as a newly mandated life-safety code. Second, capital investments designed specifically to reduce existing operating expenses, like replacing an old chiller with a high-efficiency unit.
Alex: And even in those two cases, the landlord cannot pass through the whole invoice in year one.
Morgan: Never. Those capital items must be amortized over their useful life according to generally accepted accounting principles. Furthermore, for cost-saving improvements, your lease should state that the annual amortization passed through to tenants can never exceed the actual, verified annual savings generated by that improvement. If the new chiller saves twenty thousand dollars a year in utility costs, the landlord cannot bill you thirty thousand dollars a year in amortization.
Alex: What about property management fees?
Morgan: Landlords often try to calculate the property management fee as a percentage of total operating expenses, which creates a perverse incentive to let operating costs balloon. Management fees should be capped at three to four percent of gross building revenues, or excluded from CAM entirely if you are paying full base rent.
Alex: Finally, all of these protections are useless without enforcement. What should our audit rights look like?
Morgan: You need an express audit right in the lease. Require the landlord to deliver the annual reconciliation within ninety to one hundred and twenty days after the calendar year ends, complete with line-item detail and supporting invoices. You want a lookback window of at least ninety to one hundred and eighty days to review and contest the charges. If your independent audit reveals an overcharge of more than three to five percent, the landlord must refund the overcharge immediately, pay interest at the prime rate, and reimburse your full third-party audit costs.
Alex: Time for our technical check. First question: If a landlord attempts to charge an off-campus clinic for repaving the entire parking lot under CAM maintenance, how do you evaluate that charge?
Morgan: That is a capital expenditure, not maintenance. Repaving extends the useful life of the asset, so it must be capitalized on the landlord's balance sheet. Unless the repaving directly reduces recurring operating expenses or was mandated by a brand-new post-lease code compliance requirement, it is completely excluded. Even if it qualifies under an exception, it must be amortized over its useful life, not billed as an annual expense.
Alex: Second question: In year three of a gross lease with a base year, the building's occupancy rises from seventy percent to ninety-five percent. The landlord presents an operating expense reconciliation where year three utilities are grossed up to ninety-five percent, but the base year remains at actual seventy percent numbers. What is the operational impact?
Morgan: The landlord is manufacturing artificial inflation. By leaving the base year at actual low-occupancy levels and grossing up the comparison year to full occupancy, the landlord forces the tenant to pay for the natural utility usage of other incoming tenants. You must reject the reconciliation and require the landlord to recalculate the base year grossed up to that exact same ninety-five percent occupancy level before computing any escalation.
Alex: Spot on. Here is today's Director's rule of thumb: Insist on non-cumulative caps on controllable CAM, consistent base-year gross-up, and explicit capital-expenditure exclusions. That is Day 2 of Director's Desk. We will see you tomorrow.
