Alex: You walk into a real estate committee meeting, and leadership is reviewing two proposals for a new ten-thousand-square-foot outpatient clinic. Proposal A is twenty-four dollars a square foot. Proposal B is thirty-five dollars a square foot. The chief financial officer points at Proposal A and asks why anyone would even consider Proposal B when it costs eleven dollars more per foot. The problem is, Proposal A is triple net, and Proposal B is full-service gross with a base year stop. On day one, their actual cash cost is identical, and over five years, the cheaper headline rent might actually be the more expensive deal. This is Director's Desk, Day 1.
Morgan: That scenario plays out in institutional boardrooms every single week. When people transition from corporate finance or general operations into portfolio leadership, headline rent is the first trap. Headline rent is just the marketing number. What matters to your income statement and your balance sheet is the net effective occupancy cost, and you cannot calculate that until you understand how the lease structure moves operating expenses between the landlord and the institution.
Alex: Let us lay down the three standard lease structures every director needs to evaluate: Triple Net, Full-Service Gross, and Modified Gross. Start with Triple Net, because that is where most healthcare and standalone academic leases land.
Morgan: Triple Net, or NNN, means the tenant pays a negotiated base rent, plus its pro-rata share of three operational buckets: real estate taxes, property insurance, and common area maintenance, which everyone calls CAM. In a true triple net lease, base rent is purely net to the landlord. All ongoing operational costs of keeping the building open—janitorial, HVAC maintenance, security, landscaping, management fees, utilities, and parking lot sweeping—pass directly through to the tenant.
Alex: And the mechanics of how that cash actually flows are critical. The landlord estimates those operating expenses at the beginning of each calendar year. If your suite is ten thousand square feet in a one-hundred-thousand-square-foot building, your pro-rata share is ten percent. If the landlord budgets total building operating expenses at one million one hundred thousand dollars for the year, that comes out to eleven dollars a square foot. You pay your base rent of twenty-four dollars, plus one-twelfth of that eleven-dollar pass-through every month.
Morgan: That means your monthly check to the landlord is twenty-nine thousand one hundred sixty-six dollars. Twenty thousand for base rent, nine thousand one hundred sixty-six dollars for operating expense escrow. Then, in the spring of the following year, the landlord issues an annual CAM reconciliation. If actual operating expenses were twelve dollars a square foot instead of eleven, you get an invoice for an extra ten thousand dollars. If they were ten dollars, you get a credit. Under triple net, the tenant takes nearly all the operational inflation risk.
Alex: Now contrast that with Proposal B: Full-Service Gross.
Morgan: In a Full-Service Gross lease, the landlord quotes a single number—in our example, thirty-five dollars a square foot—and that rate bundles base rent and year-one operating expenses together. The landlord covers taxes, building insurance, maintenance, common area utilities, and standard suite janitorial out of that thirty-five dollars. You write one predictable check every month: twenty-nine thousand one hundred sixty-six dollars. Exactly the same monthly cash outflow in year one as the twenty-four-dollar triple net deal.
Alex: But the key difference in an institutional multi-tenant office building is the Base Year expense stop. A lot of folks outside real estate assume gross means your rent never increases for operating costs over the entire ten-year term. That is almost never true in institutional commercial leases.
Morgan: Exactly. Almost every modern full-service lease uses a Base Year expense stop. The landlord establishes Year One of the lease—or the current calendar year—as the baseline. Let us say actual operating expenses in that base year are eleven dollars a square foot. That eleven dollars is now your expense stop. In Year Two, if operating expenses climb to eleven dollars and fifty cents, the landlord pays the first eleven dollars, and you pay the fifty-cent increase on your ten thousand square feet, which is five thousand dollars for the year. The landlord absorbs the baseline; you absorb the inflation above the baseline.
Alex: Which brings us to the hybrid: Modified Gross. We see this all the time in suburban medical office buildings and flex campus properties.
Morgan: In a Modified Gross lease, the parties pick and choose which expense categories are included in the base rate and which are billed directly. The most common institutional setup is that the landlord covers base property taxes and structural building insurance up to an agreed stop, but the tenant contracts directly for its own in-suite janitorial and suite electric, or pays those utilities on a separate submeter. It gives the institution direct control over the expenses it can actually manage—like how many nights a week the cleaning crew comes in or how long the clinic runs lights and air conditioning—while keeping base building operations on the landlord.
Alex: Let us look at the financial modeling. If a ten-thousand-square-foot suite at twenty-four dollars NNN with eleven dollars in operating expenses costs thirty-five dollars all-in in year one, and the full-service quote is thirty-five dollars with an eleven-dollar base year stop, how do you decide between them over a five- or ten-year pro forma?
Morgan: You have to look at the growth vectors and management exposure. In the triple net lease, your base rent typically escalates by a fixed percentage—say, two and a half or three percent a year—and your operating expenses escalate based on actual market inflation. If municipal property taxes reassess or property insurance spikes twenty percent because of regional storm damage, your operating expense pass-through immediately jumps from eleven dollars to thirteen dollars. You absorb every penny of that volatility. In the full-service lease, your base rent might also escalate by three percent, but your expense exposure is only the delta above the eleven-dollar stop. The landlord is writing the primary check for the baseline eleven dollars, year after year.
Alex: That leads straight into the subtle trap that blindsides even experienced directors: the under-occupied base year.
Morgan: This is the single most expensive oversight in institutional lease administration. Imagine you sign a full-service lease in a newly renovated building that is only fifty percent leased during your Base Year. Because the building is half empty, actual operating expenses for that year are artificially low—say, seven dollars a square foot instead of eleven, because janitorial, utility usage, and trash removal are minimal. The landlord sets your base year stop at seven dollars. In Year Two, the building reaches ninety-five percent occupancy. Operating expenses naturally jump to eleven dollars. Now, the landlord bills you four dollars a square foot in operating expense escalations, even though general inflation was only three percent. On a ten-thousand-square-foot lease, you just took an unexpected forty-thousand-dollar hit to your operating budget because the base year was depressed.
Alex: And the contractual fix for that is a standard gross-up provision. You must require in the lease that variable operating expenses in the base year, and in all subsequent years, are grossed up to what they would have been had the building been ninety-five or one hundred percent occupied. That establishes a realistic baseline so you do not pay for the lease-up of the rest of the property.
Morgan: That is non-negotiable. If the landlord will not agree to an occupancy gross-up on variable expenses, your base year stop is essentially a financial landmine.
Alex: Let us run a quick check to make sure these concepts are locked in. First question: why would an institutional tenant actively prefer a full-service gross lease over a triple net lease in an older, multi-tenant suburban asset?
Morgan: How do you read that one, Alex?
Alex: In an older multi-tenant building, operating expenses are highly volatile. You have aging mechanical systems, unpredictable repair bills, and fluctuating utility efficiencies. A full-service structure with an audited base year stop caps your operational exposure. The landlord takes the risk of base-level operating volatility, and the institution gets budget predictability.
Morgan: Right. You isolate your risk to net inflation over the base stop, rather than paying for every emergency mechanical repair as a maintenance pass-through. Now question two: you are negotiating a triple net lease, and the landlord's broker says CAM includes capital replacements amortized over their useful life. What is your immediate red line?
Alex: Capital expenditures that belong to the landlord's asset value, like replacing a roof or a central chiller, must be excluded from CAM entirely, or strictly limited to demonstrated, audited operational cost-saving improvements, amortized over the actual payback period. If the landlord replaces a thirty-year-old roof membrane, that is an owner capital obligation, not an operating expense passed through to an institutional tenant.
Morgan: Exactly right. Never let capital asset depreciation masquerade as operating expense pass-throughs. Here is your Director's rule of thumb for today: Always convert competing quotes to five-year all-in occupancy cost per rentable and usable square foot before comparing headline rents.
