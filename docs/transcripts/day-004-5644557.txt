Alex: We have a fifteen thousand square foot outpatient clinic lease expiring in eighteen months. Our incumbent landlord wants to renew us at twenty-six dollars a square foot triple net, with ten dollars in operating expenses escalating at three percent annually, three percent rent bumps, and fifteen dollars a foot in refresh tenant improvements. Then our broker presents an alternative: a new medical building across town offering thirty-four dollars a square foot full-service gross, two and a half percent annual escalations, four months of free rent, and a forty-five dollar tenant improvement allowance. A junior analyst looks at thirty-four dollars gross versus thirty-six dollars all-in triple net, sees four months of free rent, and thinks relocating is an obvious win. This is Director's Desk, Day 4.
Morgan: That junior analyst is looking at face rates instead of net effective cash flows and capital outlay. In institutional real estate, comparing a triple net lease directly to a full-service gross lease without stripping down the operating expense mechanics will lead you straight into a bad board presentation. Let us dissect what is actually happening in both options.
Alex: Start with the baseline economics of staying put. Option A is fifteen thousand square feet at twenty-six dollars triple net, plus ten dollars of controllable and non-controllable operating expenses.
Morgan: In Year 1, that is thirty-six dollars a foot total occupancy cost. On fifteen thousand square feet, that comes out to exactly five hundred forty thousand dollars in annual rent and pass-throughs. The landlord is giving us fifteen dollars a foot for a cosmetic refresh, which is two hundred twenty-five thousand dollars. In a clinical space, fifteen dollars covers fresh paint, commercial vinyl flooring, and LED lighting upgrades. We can phase that work over weekends. There is zero downtime, zero moving cost, and zero patient leakage.
Alex: Now walk through the relocation option. Thirty-four dollars gross sounds cheaper on day one than thirty-six dollars all-in triple net, especially with four months of abated rent.
Morgan: Here is where the hidden costs live. First, thirty-four dollars full-service gross almost always comes with a base-year expense stop. That means Year 1 operating expenses are baked into the thirty-four dollars, but starting in Year 2, any increase in building taxes, insurance, and maintenance over that base year gets billed back to us. Second, look at the capital. They are offering forty-five dollars a square foot in tenant improvements. But taking raw shell or second-generation space and turning it into a compliant clinical suite today costs at least sixty-five dollars a square foot. That creates a twenty-dollar per square foot capital deficit.
Alex: On fifteen thousand feet, a twenty-dollar gap is three hundred thousand dollars of institutional capital out of pocket before we even see our first patient.
Morgan: Exactly. Plus, moving physical assets, specialized medical equipment, re-cabling, server migration, and decommission costs on the old space run another ten dollars a foot, or one hundred fifty thousand dollars. So before Year 1 is over, relocating demands four hundred fifty thousand dollars in cash capital expenditures.
Alex: Let us look at Year 1 net cash out the door for both options. How do they compare when we factor in the concessions and the capital?
Morgan: For Option A, renewing in place, Year 1 cash outflow is simply the rent and operating expenses: five hundred forty thousand dollars. For Option B, the base rent is thirty-four dollars across fifteen thousand feet, which is five hundred ten thousand dollars annually. But we get four months of free rent, so we only pay eight months of rent in Year 1. That is three hundred forty thousand dollars in rent paid. However, add the three hundred thousand dollar tenant improvement shortfall and the one hundred fifty thousand dollars for moving and IT. Year 1 cash out the door for Option B is seven hundred ninety thousand dollars.
Alex: Relocating costs two hundred fifty thousand dollars more cash in Year 1, despite four months of free rent. But what happens across the full seven-year lease term? Does the lower annual escalation on Option B, two and a half percent versus three percent, bridge that gap?
Morgan: It does not. Over seven years, Option A's base rent and operating expenses escalate at three percent, resulting in a total nominal cash outlay of roughly four point one four million dollars. In Option B, even with the four months free and slower escalations, when you add the base-year expense pass-throughs starting in Year 2 and that upfront four hundred fifty thousand dollars in capital, your total seven-year cash outlay hits roughly four point three million dollars.
Alex: And when you run the net present value at our standard six percent institutional hurdle rate, the front-loaded capital in Option B hurts even more.
Morgan: It severely penalizes Option B. Discounted at six percent, Option A has a net present value of roughly three point three million dollars. Option B has a net present value of roughly three point six million dollars. Relocating destroys three hundred thousand dollars of economic value in present-value terms, before you even calculate the risk of clinical downtime, lost provider billing, or patients struggling to find the new facility.
Alex: So the spreadsheet says stay. But as an institutional director, you do not just sign the incumbent landlord's first renewal offer. We have eighteen months of runway. How do you leverage Option B's existence to improve Option A?
Morgan: You never tell the incumbent landlord you are afraid of moving costs. You treat the alternative building as an aggressive, viable threat. We issue a formal counter-proposal to our current landlord. We point out that modern space across the submarket is offering significant tenant improvement packages and months of free rent. We tell them that to bypass an institutional relocation, we need three concessions: bring the base rent down from twenty-six dollars to twenty-five dollars, grant us three months of base rent abatement on the renewal term, and raise the refresh allowance from fifteen dollars to twenty-five dollars a square foot so we can update the exam room casework.
Alex: From a healthcare compliance standpoint, if this space houses employed physicians or private practitioners who refer to our health system, does pushing for extra tenant improvements or lower rent create a Fair Market Value issue under Stark Law or the Anti-Kickback Statute?
Morgan: You have to keep the entire package within substantiated Fair Market Value. Any institutional lease involving referral sources requires an independent third-party Fair Market Value appraisal. That appraisal establishes the defensible range for base rent, concessions, and tenant allowances. As long as our negotiated terms sit inside that range, we remain fully compliant. Furthermore, from a financial accounting perspective under FASB ASC 842, keeping our tenant improvement allowance aligned with actual leasehold improvements prevents unexpected balance sheet capitalization discrepancies.
Alex: If the landlord accepts our counter, the net present value of renewing drops another two hundred thousand dollars, widening the advantage of staying in place to over half a million dollars. What does the final recommendation to the Vice President look like?
Morgan: The memo recommends approving a seven-year renewal in place under negotiated terms. The rationale states clearly: renewing protects five hundred forty thousand dollars in annual net operating cash flow, avoids four hundred fifty thousand dollars in unrecoverable move-related capital expenses, preserves existing clinical market share, and outperforms the competitive relocation alternative by more than three hundred thousand dollars on a discounted net present value basis.
Alex: Clean, defensible, and driven by total net effective cost rather than face rent. What is the single rule of thumb every director must remember from this deal?
Morgan: Never compare two lease proposals on base rent alone; always factor out-of-pocket capital expenditures above tenant allowance, moving and technology expenses, and operational downtime directly into your net present value model.
